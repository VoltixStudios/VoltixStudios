Paper Squadron

Capture. Defend. Dominate.

Fold a paper plane, launch it off the desk, and fly.

An arcade flight game about a squadron of paper aircraft fighting across a world made of stationery — a classroom of notebook mountains, a pirate bay of cardboard ships, and a frozen range of paper peaks.

Tilt your phone to fly, or switch to touch steering if you prefer. Press and hold the right side of the screen to fire.

Get it on Google Play

  • Three worlds

    Classroom Skies, Pirate Bay and Frost Peaks, each with its own scenery, music, boss and signature aircraft.

  • Missions that change

    Escort a balloon, hunt down an ace, run gates against the clock, collect cargo, hold out against elite waves, or take out ground bases. Every flight draws a different sequence.

  • Bosses with teeth

    Multi-stage fights that turn nastier as their armour comes off.

  • Build your plane

    Spend folds on durability, speed, agility and luck — and on firepower and fire rate for weapons from the Dart to the Cryo Cannon.

  • Wingmen

    Bring up to two escorts to fly on your flank.

  • Weekly survival

    An endless, steadily harder run in a world that rotates every week, with global ranks — and a supply drop waiting for everyone who flew it.

CoreWard

Descend. Survive. Restore.

Drill down. Read the collapse before it happens.

The surface has cooled past habitability. The last settlement survives on geothermal tap lines, and those lines are failing. You pilot single-use survey drones down through the crust, mapping strata and reaching viable heat pockets before the drone cooks.

A vertical-descent arcade puzzler in portrait, played with one thumb, in runs of two to five minutes.

  • Drilling

    Move into a block to drill it. Down is fast, sideways slower, up slowest and rarely correct.

  • Gravity

    Unsupported blocks fall after a beat, and the beat shortens with depth. Being underneath one when it lands ends the run — reading collapses before they happen is the whole skill.

  • Seams

    Drilling takes the entire connected body of that mineral, not just the cell under the bit. The hole is as large as the body you chose to bite into.

  • Fusion

    Four or more of the same mineral in contact fuse and vanish — which is not always what you want. Colour matching becomes a routing decision.

  • Heat

    The gauge is the clock. It climbs faster the deeper you go, and coolant caches are scattered thin and never in the middle column.

  • The rules stay honest

    Nothing in the economy can extend the fall delay. Reading a collapse is the skill, and it is not for sale.

Loom

Every day, one pattern.

You know all the moves. Can you find the order?

A plain woven cloth, the pattern it must become, and the four moves that get there: slide a row, swap two columns, mirror the cloth, turn it. Every move is given. What is not given is the order. Five attempts, one puzzle a day, the same for everyone in the world, and a small grid of squares to share that spoils nothing.

Two to five minutes, one thumb, no words to read. Warm, quiet, and finished on the bus.

⟶

  • One cloth a day

    The puzzle is a pure function of the date. Everyone weaves the same cloth, and the only way to play again is tomorrow.

  • Order, not content

    Every operation is on the table from the first second. Nothing to find, nothing to guess at: only the sequence is hidden, and each weave tells you which slots were right.

  • Harder by weekday

    Monday is plain. Midweek the loom says only how many slots were right, not which. Friday hides a decoy tile that belongs nowhere. Saturday has five slots. Sunday has all of it on a bigger cloth.

  • The quilt

    Every day's final cloth, solved or not, is sewn into your quilt, a month at a time. Thirty days is a quilt, and it can be shared as a picture.

  • A streak that never threatens

    It counts days played, not days solved. A lost day keeps it. Nothing ever tells you not to break it.

  • Nothing to buy to play

    The daily cloth, the last week of the archive, a free hint a day, the statistics and the quilt are free. Loom Full, once, buys every cloth ever published and no advertising, ever.

Dungeon Gourmand

Delve. Cook. Feed.

Your dragon is what you feed it, and what you feed it decides where you can go.

You are a retired adventurer keeping a hearth on the edge of the frontier, and in your care is an egg. You hire a party, send it into a dungeon, and put the phone down. Hours later it walks back in with ingredients, gold, injuries — and a written account of what happened down there.

Then you cook what they brought home, and what you cook is what the dragon becomes. The dragon flies out to find the next dungeon, and how far it can look is what decides where the next party may be sent.

An idle dungeon-crawler and dragon-raising hybrid, played in portrait in a few minutes a day.

  • Absence is the mechanic

    Nothing permanent is ever lost to time. No spoilage, no decay that costs a stage, no streak that dies while you are away. The wait is the game, never the penalty for having a life.

  • The return is the story

    Every expedition comes home as a Ledger — a written account generated from that run's own stored seed, so the rolls printed are the rolls that happened and the loot listed is the loot in the haul.

  • The kitchen

    Ingredients become dishes, and a timing tap decides how well. Cook the same recipe often enough and mastery raises its floor.

  • Who you send

    Delvers, shields, scouts and healers, hired off a tavern board that turns over every six hours. Each carries a trait, a favourite dish and a lead of their own.

  • A drawn map

    The Valley, the Mountains and the Coast, with fog that only ever lifts. The dragon flies to look; the party walks to whatever it found.

  • The rules are not for sale

    Money buys time and identity — a second party, a colour, a bag of Scales. It never buys a success rate, a loot roll or a skipped timer.

The studio

Small team, finished games

Voltix Studios is an independent studio working on Android. We build in Unity, ship in small increments, and would rather cut a feature than launch a game that argues with the player.

  • Designed before it is coded

    Each game starts as a written design document — the loop, the economy and the difficulty curve settled on paper before the first line of C#.

  • The simulation is separate

    Game logic is kept apart from rendering, so a whole run can be played headlessly in a test. A rendering bug can never become a gameplay bug.

  • The rules are not for sale

    Purchases buy convenience and cosmetics. They never buy the thing the game is actually asking you to get good at.
